Building to the GCB Standard

What Maximum Dual Exposure and the Pier Community's Storm History Mean for Deck Construction

The Garden City Pier has been rebuilt — partially or fully — multiple times through the community's history. Each rebuild reflects the coastal engineering lesson of the previous storm: better materials, better connections, better understanding of what the forces here actually require. Garden City Beach residential deck construction benefits from the same evolution in understanding. The deck built in the 1990s with zinc fasteners and standard construction represents the knowledge of that era. The deck built today should reflect what the community has learned about what this specific coastal environment actually does to materials over time.

Zinc-plated hardware at Garden City Beach's maximum dual-exposure position fails on a timeline that makes it economically irrational compared to stainless: joist hangers showing rust bleed-through at 18 to 24 months, deck screws requiring replacement within 8 to 10 years, post bases showing structural corrosion by year 12. The stainless premium on a standard deck — $350 to $600 — is recovered in the first avoided early repair cycle. This is the calculation that the pier community's storm and maintenance experience has taught its more experienced property owners, and the specification standard we apply to every Garden City Beach deck.

Composite decking adds the storm resilience advantage that wood cannot match: composite doesn't absorb the water from storm surge or flooding events. Wood can warp, swell, and develop structural compromise from storm moisture exposure. Composite dries without damage. In a community that periodically experiences the storm events that its pier's history represents, this is a material property that directly affects long-term deck performance.
