Centipede Grass in Horry County, SC — The Low-Maintenance Option Explained

Centipede grass is Horry County's low-maintenance warm-season option — a slow-growing, low-fertility grass that, when placed correctly and left relatively alone, produces a consistently maintained lawn with the least ongoing input of any warm-season species. It's not the right choice for every property, and it's frequently mismanaged by homeowners who apply the same care intensity that Bermuda requires. Understand what Centipede actually needs — and equally important, what it doesn't — and it will perform reliably for decades in the right locations throughout coastal SC.

What Makes Centipede Different

Centipede is unique among the four common Horry County warm-season grasses in one critical way: it performs better when managed less aggressively, not more. Centipede is a light feeder — over-fertilizing it produces excessive thatch, weakens the grass, and creates the "Centipede decline" syndrome that many Horry County homeowners encounter when they fertilize their Centipede lawn the same way they would a Bermuda lawn.

Centipede prefers the acid sandy soils common throughout Horry County (pH 5.0–6.0) and actually performs poorly when soil pH is raised above 6.5. Many Myrtle Beach homeowners inadvertently raise their soil pH by applying too much lime — the standard recommendation for many lawn grasses but potentially harmful for Centipede. Test soil pH before applying any amendments to a Centipede lawn.

Mowing Centipede in Myrtle Beach

Mow Centipede at 1.5–2.5 inches. Centipede's slower growth rate means mowing frequency is every 10–14 days during peak season — less demanding than Bermuda and similar to or slightly less frequent than Zoysia. Because Centipede grows more slowly and is mowed less frequently, it's a practical choice for properties where the owner wants minimal involvement in lawn management.

The most common Centipede mistake in Horry County

Over-fertilizing. Centipede needs 1–2 pounds of actual nitrogen per 1,000 square feet annually — total, for the entire year. Bermuda may need 4–6 pounds. Applying Bermuda-level fertility to Centipede is the most direct path to Centipede decline, excessive thatch, and eventual lawn failure. Less is genuinely more with this grass.

Where Centipede Works and Where It Doesn't

Good Centipede situations: Low-traffic residential properties where minimal input is the goal. Properties with acid sandy soil in the natural Horry County pH range of 5.0–6.0. Homeowners who want a maintained appearance without weekly mowing during peak season. Secondary lots and less-trafficked yard areas.

Poor Centipede situations: High-traffic vacation rentals — Centipede's traffic recovery is poor and damaged areas recover slowly. HOA communities with active height enforcement — Centipede's slower growth makes compliance easier, but the grass's thinning under any traffic pressure creates appearance problems over time. Properties with alkaline soil or heavy lime application history.
